Before reading this post, you can think about these questions.

"When an LLM learns one training response, Does it only increase the probability of that exact response?"

"Why can supervised finetuning unintentionally reinforce unrelated facts, phrases, or hallucinations?"

"Why does off-policy DPO sometimes decrease the likelihood of not only the rejected response, but also the chosen response?"

"Can SFT, DPO, IPO, SPIN, and other post-training algorithms be understood through one common mathematical framework?"

📓 **Paper**: Yi Ren, Danica J. Sutherland (2025). _Learning Dynamics of LLM Finetuning_. ICLR 2025 Oral / Outstanding Paper. The paper was selected as one of three outstanding papers among 3,704 accepted ICLR 2025 papers.


# **I. SHORT SUMMARY**

_Learning Dynamics of LLM Finetuning_ studies what happens to an LLM's output distribution **during training**, rather than analyzing only the final trained model.

The central question is:

After training the model on one example $(x_u,y_u)$, how does its prediction on another input $x_o$ change?

The paper decomposes this prediction change into three components:

1. **Adaptation term** $\mathcal{A}_t$ Describes how a change in logits becomes a change in log-probabilities.
2. **Kernel term** $\mathcal{K}_t$ Measures how strongly the updated training example interacts with the observed example through parameter gradients.
3. **Residual term** $\mathcal{G}_t$ Describes the direction and magnitude of the learning signal generated by the loss.

Using this framework, the paper explains several otherwise counterintuitive behaviors:

- SFT increases the likelihood of the target response.
- SFT can initially increase the likelihood of semantically or parametrically similar responses.
- These indirectly reinforced responses can include phrases or facts that were never correct for the current prompt.
- DPO applies a positive signal to the chosen response and a negative signal to the rejected response.
- When the rejected response is already very unlikely, its large negative update can suppress a broad region of output space.
- The remaining probability mass becomes concentrated on a small number of responses.
- This phenomenon is called the **squeezing effect**.
- Under prolonged off-policy DPO, even the likelihood of the chosen response can eventually decrease.

The paper therefore presents a unified dynamical interpretation of SFT, DPO, IPO, SPIN, SPPO, SLiC, and several related post-training methods.


# **II. MATHEMATICAL BACKGROUND**

These concepts are useful for understanding the paper.


## **II-1. Gradient Descent as a Dynamical System**

Suppose that the model has parameters $\theta_t$ at training step $t$.

Given a training example $(x_u,y_u)$, one gradient-descent update is

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(x_u,y_u;\theta_t)$$

where $\eta$ is the learning rate.

Most conventional analyses ask "Where does $\theta_t$ converge?"

This paper asks a different question.

**How does each individual update change the model's prediction on every possible input and response?**

For another observed input $x_o$, we therefore examine

$$\Delta \log \pi_t(y\mid x_o) = \log \pi_{\theta_{t+1}}(y\mid x_o) - \log \pi_{\theta_t}(y\mid x_o)$$

This quantity measures how training on $(x_u,y_u)$ changes the model's confidence in response $y$ for input $x_o$.


## **II-2. First-Order Taylor Approximation**

For a sufficiently small update, the change in the model prediction can be approximated using a first-order Taylor expansion:

$$\Delta\log \pi_t(y\mid x_o) \approx \nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta_t}(y\mid x_o)^\top \Delta\theta_t$$

Using

$$\Delta\theta_t = -\eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(x_u,y_u;\theta_t)$$

we obtain

$$\Delta\log \pi_t(y\mid x_o) \approx -\eta \nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta_t}(y\mid x_o)^\top \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(x_u,y_u;\theta_t)$$

This already gives an influence-like interpretation:

- If the two gradients point in similar directions, training on $(x_u,y_u)$ strongly affects the prediction on $x_o$.
- If they are nearly orthogonal, the influence is small.
- If they point in opposing directions, training can decrease the observed prediction.

However, the paper decomposes this interaction more finely at the **logit level**.

---

## **II-3. Softmax and Log-Probability**

Let

$$z_\theta(x)\in\mathbb{R}^V$$

be the logits over a vocabulary of size $V$, and let

$$\pi_\theta(x) = \operatorname{Softmax}(z_\theta(x))$$

For one token $y$,

$$\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) = z_{\theta,y}(x) - \log\sum_{j=1}^{V}e^{z_{\theta,j}(x)}$$

The derivative of the entire log-probability vector with respect to the logits is

$$\mathcal{A}_t(x) = \nabla_z\log\pi_{\theta_t}(x) = I-\mathbf{1}\pi_{\theta_t}(x)^\top$$

This matrix converts a change in logits into a change in log-probabilities.

It is called the **adaptation matrix** in the paper.

The important point is that $\mathcal{A}_t$ depends on the model's current probability distribution. Therefore, the same logit update can produce a different probability change depending on whether the model is uncertain, confident, or already highly concentrated.


## **II-4. Empirical Neural Tangent Kernel**

For two inputs $x_o$ and $x_u$, the paper defines the empirical neural tangent kernel:

$$\mathcal{K}_t(x_o,x_u) = \nabla_\theta z_{\theta_t}(x_o) \nabla_\theta z_{\theta_t}(x_u)^\top$$

This is not merely a conventional semantic similarity measure.

It measures whether changing the parameters to modify the logits for $x_u$ will also modify the logits for $x_o$.

A large kernel norm

$$\left|\mathcal{K}_t(x_o,x_u)\right|_F$$

means that learning from $x_u$ is likely to strongly affect the prediction on $x_o$.

A small kernel norm means that the two examples are nearly independent under the model's current parameterization.

Therefore, $\mathcal{K}_t$ is better interpreted as a form of

**learned functional similarity**

rather than ordinary embedding similarity.

Two prompts or responses may be linguistically different but still have a large kernel interaction if the model uses similar parameters or features to process them.

## **II-5. Cross-Entropy Gradient**

For SFT, the token-level cross-entropy loss is

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}} = -\log \pi_\theta(y_u\mid x_u)$$

Let $\mathbf{e}_{y_u}$ be the one-hot vector for the target token. Then

$$\nabla_z\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}} = \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x_u)-\mathbf{e}_{y_u}$$

The paper denotes this residual learning signal by

$$\mathcal{G}_t(x_u,y_u) = \pi_{\theta_t}(\cdot\mid x_u)-\mathbf{e}_{y_u}$$

This vector points from the target one-hot distribution toward the model's current distribution.

Because gradient descent moves in the negative-gradient direction, the update:

- increases the target logit;
- decreases competing logits;
- redistributes probability mass across the entire vocabulary.

This is important: even ordinary SFT is not an isolated update to one token. It alters the whole output distribution.


# **III. A GENERAL FRAMEWORK FOR LEARNING DYNAMICS**

## **III-1. Per-Step Decomposition**

For a single-token prediction, the paper derives the approximate one-step change

$$\Delta\log \pi_t(x_o) = -\eta \mathcal{A}_t(x_o) \mathcal{K}_t(x_o,x_u) \mathcal{G}_t(x_u,y_u) + O(\eta^2)$$

This is the main equation of the paper.

Let us examine the three terms.


### **(1) Adaptation term**

$$\mathcal{A}_t(x_o) = I-\mathbf{1}\pi_{\theta_t}(x_o)^\top$$

This term reflects the current shape of the output probability distribution.

It answers:

Given a particular logit change, how will probability mass be redistributed?

Because probabilities must sum to one, increasing one output generally requires decreasing others.


### **(2) Kernel term**

$$\mathcal{K}_t(x_o,x_u) = \nabla_\theta z_{\theta_t}(x_o) \nabla_\theta z_{\theta_t}(x_u)^\top$$

This term determines how strongly the training example $x_u$ influences the observed example $x_o$.

It answers:

Does the model use overlapping parameters or features for these two examples?

If the kernel interaction is strong, learning one example can substantially change the other.



### **(3) Residual term**

$$\mathcal{G}_t(x_u,y_u) = \nabla_z\mathcal{L}(x_u,y_u)$$

This term depends on the finetuning objective.

It answers:

In which output-space direction does the loss want the model to move?

SFT, DPO, IPO, SPIN, and other methods differ primarily through this residual term and through the examples on which it is evaluated.

The paper illustrates this first on MNIST: learning an image of the digit 4 directly increases confidence in 4, but may also increase confidence in 9 for images that are represented similarly by the network. This toy example motivates the corresponding interactions among LLM responses.



## **III-2. Sequence-Level Decomposition**

An LLM response consists of multiple tokens:

$$y=(y_1,\ldots,y_L)$$

Its log-probability is

$$\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \sum_{l=1}^{L} \log \pi_\theta \left( y_l \mid x,y_{<l} \right)$$

Each token is evaluated under a different context

$$\chi_l=(x,y_{<l})$$

Therefore, the update to a sequence is a sum of token-level interactions:

$$\Delta \log \pi_t(y\mid x_o) \approx -\eta \sum_{m=1}^{L_o} \sum_{l=1}^{L_u} \mathcal{A}_t(\chi_{o,m}) \mathcal{K}_t(\chi_{o,m},\chi_{u,l}) \mathcal{G}_t(\chi_{u,l},y_{u,l})$$

This is one reason LLM finetuning dynamics are substantially more complicated than ordinary classification:

- every response contains many training targets;
- every token uses a different prefix;
- every token can interact with every token of another response;
- these effects accumulate across batches and epochs.



# **IV. LEARNING DYNAMICS OF SFT**

## **IV-1. SFT Objective**

For a prompt $x_u$ and target response $y_u^+$, the SFT loss is

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}} = - \sum_{l=1}^{L} \log \pi_\theta \left( y_{u,l}^+ \mid x_u,y_{u,<l}^+ \right)$$

At each token position,

$$\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{SFT}}_{t,l} = \pi_{\theta_t}(\cdot\mid\chi_{u,l}^+) - \mathbf{e}_{y_{u,l}^+}$$

As expected, the most direct effect is to increase the probability of $y_u^+$.

But this is not the only effect.



## **IV-2. Direct Pull-Up and Indirect Pull-Up**

Suppose the model learns the pair

$$(x_u,y_u^+)$$

For the exact response $y_u^+$, the update produces a strong **direct pull-up**.

However, consider another response $y'$ that has a strong kernel interaction with $y_u^+$:

$$\left| \mathcal{K}_t(\chi',\chi_u^+) \right|_F \text{ is large}$$

Then $y'$ may also be indirectly pulled up, even though it never appears as a target.

Therefore, SFT does not simply teach:

"For prompt $x_u$, generate $y_u^+$."

It can also teach:

"Increase outputs that share related features, tokens, styles, facts, or parameter directions with $y_u^+$."

This explains why rephrasings or similar responses can initially become more likely.



## **IV-3. Probability Conservation and Push-Down Pressure**

There is a second force.

Because softmax probabilities sum to one,

$$\sum_y \pi_\theta(y\mid x)=1$$

increasing the target probability necessarily pushes down other probabilities.

Therefore, a response $y'\neq y_u^+$ is subject to two competing pressures:

1. **Indirect pull-up pressure**

$$\mathcal{K}_t(y',y_u^+)$$

can increase its likelihood if it is sufficiently similar to the target.

2. **Normalization push-down pressure**

The increase in the target response removes probability mass from competing responses.

This gives rise to a characteristic trajectory:

$$\text{initial increase} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{turning point} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{eventual decrease}$$

At the start of training, similarity-based pull-up can dominate.

Later, as the exact target becomes increasingly probable, the competition for probability mass becomes stronger, and most non-target responses decline.

The experiments show this behavior for:

- rephrasings of the preferred response;
- preferred answers for other prompts;
- generic human-written sentences;
- unrelated responses with partially shared linguistic structure.

The precise turning point depends on how strongly each response interacts with the supervised target.


## **IV-4. A Possible Explanation for Hallucination Amplification**

Suppose the training data contains:

$$x_B \rightarrow y_B$$

and response $y_B$ contains a factual phrase $f_B$.

Now consider a different prompt $x_A$.

If the model's internal representation produces a strong interaction between the response contexts of $x_A$ and $x_B$, training on $y_B$ may increase the probability of $f_B$ under $x_A$ as well.

Schematically,

$$(x_B,y_B) \overset{\mathrm{SFT}}{\longrightarrow} \text{increase }p(f_B\mid x_B)$$

but also

$$\mathcal{K}_t(x_A,x_B)\text{ large} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{increase }p(f_B\mid x_A)$$

The model may then reuse a fact, phrase, or answer pattern learned for question B when answering question A.

The paper does not claim that this completely explains all hallucination. Rather, it proposes a plausible learning-dynamics mechanism for specific forms of hallucination amplification and repetitive generation during finetuning.


# **V. LEARNING DYNAMICS OF DPO**

## **V-1. DPO Objective**

A DPO training example consists of

$$(x_u,y_u^+,y_u^-)$$

where:

- $y_u^+$ is the chosen response;
- $y_u^-$ is the rejected response;
- $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the reference policy.

The DPO loss is

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta) = - \log\sigma \left[ \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_u^+\mid x_u)} {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_u^+\mid x_u)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_u^-\mid x_u)} {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_u^-\mid x_u)} \right]$$

Define the policy-reference preference margin

$$h_\theta = \beta \left[ \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_u^+\mid x_u)} {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_u^+\mid x_u)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_u^-\mid x_u)} {\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_u^-\mid x_u)} \right]$$

Let

$$a=\sigma(h_\theta)$$

When $a$ is close to one, the current policy already separates the chosen and rejected responses well.

When $a$ is small, the current policy has not yet learned the desired preference.



## **V-2. Positive and Negative Residuals**

The paper decomposes DPO into two SFT-like residual signals:

$$\mathcal{G}^t_{\mathrm{DPO}+} = \beta(1-a) \left[ \pi_{\theta_t}(\cdot\mid\chi_u^+) - \mathbf{e}_{y_u^+} \right]$$

and

$$\mathcal{G}^t_{\mathrm{DPO}-} = \beta(1-a) \left[ \pi_{\theta_t}(\cdot\mid\chi_u^-) - \mathbf{e}_{y_u^-} \right]$$

The resulting dynamics contain two competing terms:

$$\Delta\log \pi_t(x_o) \approx -\eta\mathcal{A}_t(x_o) \left[ \mathcal{K}_t(x_o,\chi_u^+) \mathcal{G}^t_{\mathrm{DPO}+} - \mathcal{K}_t(x_o,\chi_u^-) \mathcal{G}^t_{\mathrm{DPO}-} \right]$$

The first term pulls up the chosen response.

The second term pushes down the rejected response.

This looks simple, but the negative update behaves very differently from the positive one.



## **V-3. The Role of the DPO Margin**

The factor

$$\beta(1-a)$$

controls the strength of both updates.

If the current model does not sufficiently prefer $y^+$ over $y^-$, then $a$ is small, so the update is strong.

If the preference is already well separated, $a$ approaches one, so the update weakens.

Thus DPO automatically reduces the learning signal for examples on which the model has already achieved a sufficiently large preference margin.

This observation also helps place several preference-optimization algorithms in a common framework:

- DPO and IPO use closely related update directions but different scalar strengths.
- Margin-based methods activate or deactivate updates depending on the preference gap.
- On-policy methods alter the region of model probability space from which $y^-$ is sampled.
- SPIN and self-play methods continually change the negative distribution.
- SPPO and related methods constrain how the chosen and rejected likelihoods move.

The important distinction is not only the algebraic loss function.

It is also:

Where are the chosen and rejected responses located under the current policy?



# **VI. THE SQUEEZING EFFECT**

## **VI-1. Why Pushing Down an Unlikely Response Is Dangerous**

Suppose the rejected response $y^-$ already has extremely low probability.

Intuitively, we might expect that decreasing it further should have little effect.

However, a large negative gradient on an already unlikely output can affect a much broader region of output space.

Consider a simplified output distribution:

$$\pi_\theta(y^-) \approx 0$$

The negative DPO update tries to move probability away from $y^-$.

But there is almost no probability mass left on $y^-$ itself.

Through the adaptation matrix and kernel interactions, the negative signal can instead decrease the confidence of many responses similar to $y^-$, or even many responses in the surrounding functional region.

The suppressed probability mass must go somewhere because probabilities sum to one.

It is therefore concentrated on the response that already has the largest probability, denoted $y^\ast$.

This leads to:

$$\text{many outputs pushed down} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{mass concentrated on }y^\ast$$

The output distribution becomes increasingly sharp or peaked.

The paper calls this the **squeezing effect**.



## **VI-2. Geometric Intuition**

Imagine the model's output distribution as a landscape.

- High-probability responses lie on peaks.
- Low-probability responses lie in valleys.

In off-policy DPO, the preference pairs are collected in advance, often from another model or a human-written dataset.

As the current policy changes, a fixed rejected response may move deeper into a low-probability valley.

DPO nevertheless continues to apply a negative update to that response.

Instead of making a meaningful local distinction between $y^+$ and $y^-$, the update can compress a broad low-probability region.

The probability mass is then squeezed toward a small number of existing peaks.

Consequences may include:

- decreased output diversity;
- repetitive phrases;
- sharper token distributions;
- collapse toward greedy-decoding outputs;
- unexpected declines in chosen-response likelihood.



## **VI-3. Why the Chosen Response Can Also Decrease**

DPO directly encourages the relative preference

$$\log\pi_\theta(y^+\mid x) - \log\pi_\theta(y^-\mid x)$$

It does not necessarily require that

$$\log\pi_\theta(y^+\mid x)$$

increase in absolute terms.

The preference margin can improve even when both terms decrease, as long as the rejected response decreases faster:

$$\Delta\log\pi_\theta(y^-) < \Delta\log\pi_\theta(y^+) < 0$$

For example,

$$\log\pi(y^+): -10\rightarrow -12$$

while

$$\log\pi(y^-): -11\rightarrow -20$$

The preference gap grows from

$$-10-(-11)=1$$

to

$$-12-(-20)=8$$

DPO considers this an improvement, even though the chosen response became less likely.

The squeezing-effect analysis explains how this behavior can emerge dynamically during prolonged off-policy DPO.



## **VI-4. Why On-Policy DPO Can Help**

In on-policy DPO, responses are sampled from the current model.

Therefore, the rejected response is less likely to be located in an extremely low-probability region under that same policy.

Schematically,

$$y^-\sim\pi_{\theta_t}(\cdot\mid x)$$

implies that $y^-$ generally has non-negligible probability under $\pi_{\theta_t}$.

Pushing down this response is more likely to produce a meaningful local preference correction.

By contrast, in off-policy DPO,

$$y^-\sim q_{\mathrm{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$$

and it is possible that

$$\pi_{\theta_t}(y^-\mid x)\ll q_{\mathrm{old}}(y^-\mid x)$$

The negative response may have become irrelevant to the current model, but the training objective continues to penalize it.

This gives one dynamical explanation for why on-policy preference optimization and iterative data collection can outperform fixed offline preference training.



# **VII. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP**

The paper verifies the framework using two common preference datasets:

- Anthropic-HH;
- UltraFeedback.

It conducts experiments with several model families and scales, including:

- Pythia models;
- Qwen models.

The authors construct a training set of 5,000 preference examples and a probing set containing prompts paired with multiple response categories.

For each prompt, they track the log-probability of responses such as:

1. the exact chosen response;
2. the exact rejected response;
3. rephrasings of the chosen response;
4. rephrasings of the rejected response;
5. a preferred response from another prompt;
6. an irrelevant answer to another question;
7. a generic human-written sentence;
8. randomly generated English text;
9. the initial model response;
10. the current greedy-decoding response.

Rather than measuring only final benchmark scores, they repeatedly probe these response types during training.

This enables them to observe:

$$\text{which responses rise}, \quad \text{which responses fall}, \quad \text{when the direction changes}$$

The qualitative trends appear across different datasets and model sizes, supporting the claim that the proposed decomposition captures a general training mechanism rather than a single-model artifact.



# **VIII. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS**

## **VIII-1. SFT Dynamics**

During SFT:

- The exact chosen response steadily increases.
- Rephrasings and similar responses often rise early.
- Responses for unrelated prompts can also rise if they have strong kernel interaction with the target response.
- Later, many non-target responses begin to decline because of normalization pressure.
- More strongly related responses reach their turning point later.
- Weakly related responses decline earlier.

This supports the interpretation that each response follows a competition between

$$\text{similarity-induced pull-up}$$

and

$$\text{softmax-induced push-down}$$



## **VIII-2. DPO Dynamics**

During off-policy DPO:

- The rejected response sharply decreases.
- Responses similar to the rejected response also decrease.
- Many other non-target responses can decrease together.
- The greedy or argmax response becomes increasingly dominant.
- After some point, the chosen response can also begin to decrease.
- The distribution becomes more concentrated as training continues.

This behavior is consistent with the squeezing effect.

It also implies that looking only at the preference accuracy or DPO loss can be misleading.

A model may achieve a larger chosen–rejected margin while simultaneously becoming:

- less diverse;
- less calibrated;
- less likely to produce the designated chosen response;
- more concentrated on a different dominant output.



# **IX. A SIMPLE METHOD INSPIRED BY THE ANALYSIS**

## **IX-1. Motivation**

The squeezing effect is strongest when DPO applies a large negative update to a response that is already located in a low-probability region.

The paper therefore proposes making the rejected responses less extremely unlikely before beginning DPO.

This sounds counterintuitive.

Normally, we would not deliberately train a model to increase the probability of rejected responses.

But the goal is not to make the final model prefer them.

The goal is to prevent DPO from applying an unstable negative update to responses that the model already assigns nearly zero probability.



## **IX-2. Training-Data Extension**

The proposed pipeline extends the SFT stage by including rejected responses.

Instead of training only on

$$(x,y^+)$$

the extended SFT stage also exposes the model to

$$(x,y^-)$$

This increases the initial likelihood of rejected responses before DPO.

As a result, they are no longer located as deeply in the low-probability valley when preference training begins.

DPO can then distinguish $y^+$ from $y^-$ without producing as severe a global squeezing effect.

The pipeline is approximately:

$$\text{Extended SFT on }y^+\text{ and }y^-$$

followed by

$$\text{DPO preferring }y^+\text{ over }y^-$$



## **IX-3. Why This Can Work**

The method intentionally makes the initial problem harder:

$$\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}(y^-) \text{ becomes larger}$$

But this yields a healthier DPO update because the rejected response remains in a region of probability space that the current model meaningfully represents.

The experiments show:

- weaker decay of non-target responses;
- slower concentration on the greedy response;
- a milder squeezing effect;
- improved win rates after several DPO epochs.

Before DPO, the baseline can initially be stronger because it has not been trained on rejected outputs. After DPO, however, the extended method performs better because its training trajectory is more stable.

This is an important methodological lesson:

The best initialization for preference optimization is not necessarily the model that already assigns the lowest probability to all rejected responses.

A model may benefit from keeping meaningful probability support over the outputs that preference optimization intends to compare.


# **X. UNDERSTANDING THE PAPER THROUGH AN INFORMATION-THEORETIC PERSPECTIVE**

The paper itself is primarily based on gradient dynamics and kernel interactions, not on a formal information-theoretic derivation. However, its results admit a useful information-theoretic interpretation.


## **X-1. Finetuning as Redistribution of Probability Mass**

An LLM defines a conditional distribution

$$\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$$

Finetuning does not simply attach a higher score to one response. It redistributes probability mass over a huge output space.

For a fixed prompt,

$$\sum_y\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=1$$

Therefore, increasing one family of responses implies decreasing another family.

The central issue is not only:

Which response receives supervision?

It is also:

Across which other responses does the resulting probability displacement propagate?

The kernel term $\mathcal{K}_t$ determines this propagation structure.


## **X-2. Entropy and the Squeezing Effect**

The conditional entropy of the model is

$$H_\theta(Y\mid X=x) = - \sum_y \pi_\theta(y\mid x) \log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)$$

When probability becomes concentrated on a small number of responses, entropy decreases.

The squeezing effect can therefore be interpreted as an excessive reduction in conditional output entropy:

$$H_{\theta_{t+1}}(Y\mid X=x) < H_{\theta_t}(Y\mid X=x)$$

A certain entropy reduction is desirable because alignment should eliminate undesirable outputs.

However, excessive entropy reduction can remove useful alternative responses and lead to:

- mode concentration;
- repetitive language;
- reduced diversity;
- brittle decoding;
- overconfident behavior.

From this perspective, preference tuning faces a rate-allocation-like problem:

How much probability mass should be removed from undesirable regions without destroying useful variation elsewhere?


## **X-3. Relative Preference Versus Absolute Likelihood**

DPO optimizes a relative quantity:

$$\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y^+\mid x)} {\pi_\theta(y^-\mid x)}$$

This ratio contains less information than the two absolute likelihoods separately.

Many pairs of values yield the same preference gap.

For example,

$$(\log\pi(y^+),\log\pi(y^-)) = (-2,-5)$$

and

$$(-20,-23)$$

have the same difference:

$$3$$

But they represent very different generative models.

In the second case, both responses are effectively inaccessible.

Thus a preference objective can succeed in relative discrimination while failing to preserve absolute support for high-quality responses.

This distinction is central to understanding likelihood displacement.


## **X-4. Kernel Interaction as an Information-Transfer Channel**

The kernel

$$\mathcal{K}_t(x_o,x_u)$$

can be viewed as describing a local channel through which information from training example $x_u$ transfers to prediction $x_o$.

A strong interaction means:

$$\text{information learned from }x_u$$

is likely to affect

$$\text{the model's behavior on }x_o$$

This transfer can be beneficial when the examples share a genuine structure.

It can be harmful when the model's learned similarity is spurious.

For example:

- beneficial transfer: two equivalent mathematical formulations;
- harmful transfer: two prompts sharing surface patterns but requiring different facts;
- beneficial transfer: two safe responses expressing the same policy;
- harmful transfer: refusal phrases being transferred to harmless prompts.

Thus generalization and interference are not fundamentally separate mechanisms.

They are two outcomes of the same cross-example interaction.


# **XI. WHY THIS PAPER MATTERS**

## **XI-1. It Studies the Path, Not Only the Endpoint**

Most work compares algorithms through:

- final reward;
- benchmark accuracy;
- preference win rate;
- KL divergence from a reference model;
- final chosen–rejected margin.

This paper instead studies

$${\pi_{\theta_t}}_{t=0}^{T}$$

That is, it treats the entire training trajectory as the object of analysis.

Two models with similar final loss may have followed very different trajectories and may differ in stability, diversity, and robustness.


## **XI-2. It Unifies SFT and Preference Optimization**

SFT and DPO are often presented as fundamentally different stages:

- SFT teaches desired outputs;
- DPO aligns relative preferences.

This paper shows that both can be expressed through the same basic structure:

$$-\eta\mathcal{A}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{G}$$

The main changes are:

- the residual learning signal;
- the sign of the update;
- the examples used to form the update;
- the current probability region occupied by those examples.

This makes it easier to compare new preference algorithms by asking:

1. What residual signal do they generate?
2. How large is it?
3. On which responses is it applied?
4. Where are those responses under the current policy?
5. Through which kernel interactions does the signal propagate?


## **XI-3. It Gives a Mechanistic Explanation of Off-Policy Problems**

The paper does not merely report that off-policy DPO can be unstable.

It proposes a concrete mechanism:

$$\text{fixed rejected response} \rightarrow \text{increasingly unlikely under current policy} \rightarrow \text{large negative update in a valley} \rightarrow \text{broad probability suppression} \rightarrow \text{squeezing}$$

This connects several observations:

- likelihood displacement;
- chosen likelihood decline;
- repetitive generation;
- benefits of on-policy sampling;
- benefits of constrained or regularized DPO variants.


## **XI-4. It Suggests New Data-Centric Research Questions**

The framework suggests that preference-pair quality cannot be determined only by label correctness.

A pair should potentially be evaluated by:

$$\left| \mathcal{K}_t(y^+,y^-) \right|$$

the current likelihoods

$$\pi_{\theta_t}(y^+\mid x), \qquad \pi_{\theta_t}(y^-\mid x)$$

the residual magnitude

$$\beta(1-a)$$

and the effect on other responses.

This naturally leads to questions such as:

- Which preference pairs should be used early in training?
- Which pairs should be introduced later?
- Should easy negatives be removed after they become too unlikely?
- Should preference data be refreshed on-policy?
- Can pair selection maximize useful representation change?
- Can harmful cross-example interference be predicted from the kernel?
- Can we regularize entropy or probability support during DPO?
- Can preference pairs be selected using geometric coverage of the response manifold?

These questions are highly relevant to learning-dynamics-based alignment research.


# **XII. LIMITATIONS**

The paper is conceptually strong, but several limitations should be kept in mind.

## **XII-1. First-Order Approximation**

The decomposition is based on a local Taylor approximation:

$$\Delta f \approx \nabla f^\top\Delta\theta$$

The approximation is most accurate when the learning rate and parameter update are small.

For large updates, higher-order terms may matter.


## **XII-2. Relative Stability of the Kernel**

The accumulated interpretation assumes that relative sample interactions do not change too violently during training.

This is weaker than requiring a perfectly fixed NTK, but it still imposes a degree of dynamical stability.

In strongly feature-learning regimes, the kernel itself may evolve substantially.

That kernel evolution could be an important research target rather than merely an approximation error.


## **XII-3. Kernel Similarity Is Not a Complete Semantic Explanation**

A large $\mathcal{K}_t$ tells us that two examples interact strongly through the current model.

It does not directly explain:

- which internal features are shared;
- which attention heads mediate the interaction;
- whether the interaction is semantic or superficial;
- whether it arises from prompt tokens, response tokens, or formatting.

A fuller mechanistic interpretation would require combining learning dynamics with representation analysis or mechanistic interpretability.


## **XII-4. Sequence-Level Complexity**

The exact response space is enormous.

The paper analyzes a strategically selected set of probing responses rather than the entire output distribution.

Therefore, the squeezing effect is supported through theoretically motivated probes and empirical trends, but directly characterizing its full distributional geometry remains difficult.


## **XII-5. The Proposed Method Is Deliberately Simple**

Training on rejected responses before DPO is useful as a proof of concept.

It is probably not the final solution.

More principled alternatives may include:

- adaptive negative sampling;
- likelihood-aware pair weighting;
- on-policy refresh;
- entropy constraints;
- support-preserving regularization;
- kernel-aware pair selection;
- dynamically stopping updates on exhausted negatives.



# **XIII. MY THOUGHT**

What surprised me most is that finetuning cannot be understood as merely increasing the probability of the supervised answer. 😲

Even a single SFT example creates a learning signal that propagates to many other responses through the model's parameter geometry. Therefore, generalization, hallucination amplification, style transfer, and interference can all emerge from the same underlying mechanism:

$$\text{cross-example gradient interaction}$$

I also found the distinction between **relative preference improvement** and **absolute likelihood improvement** especially important.

DPO can successfully increase

$$\log\pi_\theta(y^+\mid x) - \log\pi_\theta(y^-\mid x)$$

while decreasing both $\pi_\theta(y^+\mid x)$ and $\pi_\theta(y^-\mid x)$.

This means that a better preference margin does not necessarily imply that the model has become more capable of generating the desired response.

The squeezing effect provides an intuitive explanation for this problem. When an off-policy rejected response has already become extremely unlikely, continuing to push it down does not provide meaningful preference information. Instead, the update can remove probability mass from a broad output region and concentrate the model on a small number of dominant responses.

From a research perspective, I think the most important implication is that **preference data should not be treated as static**.

A preference pair that is useful at the beginning of training may become uninformative or even harmful later.

Therefore, the effectiveness of a pair should depend on the training state:

$$\text{Utility of pair} = f \left( \theta_t, \pi_{\theta_t}(y^+), \pi_{\theta_t}(y^-), \mathcal{K}_t, \mathcal{G}_t \right)$$

This suggests a dynamic curriculum for preference optimization:

- early training may use broad, high-confidence preference pairs;
- middle training may focus on ambiguous but informative pairs;
- late training may remove already-exhausted negatives;
- new on-policy pairs may be generated near the current decision boundary.

In that sense, this paper is not only an explanation of SFT and DPO. It gives a mathematical starting point for asking a deeper question:

**Which training example should the model learn from at each point in its trajectory, and how will that example reshape the rest of the model's output space?**
